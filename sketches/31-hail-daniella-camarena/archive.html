Archive: where did this history begin?

Three strands that keep crossing: rituals that ask for rain, instruments that measure it, and machines that try to command it.

Long before anyone fired a cannon at a cloud, people in central Mexico read the weather, named its powers and climbed mountains to ask for rain. Then came written observations, instruments and observatories. Then came machines sold to stop hail or make rain. The tiemperos and the hail cannon at VW Puebla are the latest meeting of these strands.

Who owned the rain

In the Nahua world the rain was not a resource. It belonged to someone, and had to be asked for.

Tlaloc

Nahuatl · lord of rain, lightning and hail

Tlaloc lived in the mountains, where clouds are made. He brought the rain that grew the maize, and also the hail, frost and floods that ruined it. His helpers, the tlaloque, kept the water in jars; when they struck the jars, thunder sounded and rain fell. The Mexica asked him for rain on the summit of Mount Tlaloc, east of the Valley of Mexico.

The tiemperos still climb the volcanoes, which are counted among the tlaloque, to ask for rain. See the tiemperos →

Chalchiuhtlicue

Nahuatl · “she of the jade skirt”

Tlaloc’s companion ruled the water once it reached the ground: rivers, lakes, springs and the sea. In Horton’s terms she governs everything after precipitation. Springs, where ground water comes back to the surface, were her places, and they still are places of ritual.

On 3 May the tiemperos place crosses at springs. Follow step 12 →

Tlalocan

Nahuatl · “the place of Tlaloc”

Tlaloc’s paradise, always green and always wet. People who died by water or by lightning went there. A mural at Tepantitla in Teotihuacan, painted centuries before the Mexica, is often read as an image of it.

The tiemperos are often chosen by lightning: people struck who survived. Lightning marks a bond with Tlaloc’s world.

How the strands cross

A working timeline. Each entry is tagged by strand.

  1. c. 200–650 CEritual

    Teotihuacan paints the Tlalocan murals at Tepantitla. Water, mountains and rain are already at the centre of the city’s imagery.

  2. Before 1521ritual

    The Codex Borgia, probably painted in the Puebla–Tlaxcala region, shows Tlaloc in the 260-day calendar. Rain is one of the 20 day signs, so weather is built into the count of time itself.

  3. 1500sritual

    The Mexica hold yearly rain ceremonies for Tlaloc, from the dry season to the harvest, on mountain tops and at springs and lakes.

  4. 1577science

    Bernardino de Sahagún and Nahua scholars complete the Florentine Codex. Book 7 records Nahua knowledge of the sun, moon, stars, winds, clouds, rain and hail, written down by a missionary project that was also meant to root out the old rituals.

  5. 1788–1795science

    José Antonio Alzate publishes the Gazetas de Literatura de México, a journal of natural science that includes notes on the weather of the Valley of Mexico.

  6. 1877science

    The Observatorio Meteorológico Central opens in Mexico City under Mariano Bárcena. Weather becomes a matter of the state, measured at stations and sent by telegraph.

  7. 1896capital

    In Styria, Austria, the vineyard owner Albert Stiger builds “vortex guns” to fire at hail clouds. The idea spreads across European wine regions within a few years.

  8. 1902capital

    Stiger guns are fired at the sky in Charleville, Queensland, Australia, during a drought. No rain follows, and the trial is abandoned.

  9. 1946capital

    Vincent Schaefer, working for General Electric in the United States, makes snow form in a cloud by dropping dry ice into it. Cloud seeding begins as an industrial and military technology.

  10. 1989 onwardritual

    Anthropologist Julio Glockner documents the tiemperos of Popocatépetl and Iztaccíhuatl, who still climb to caves and crosses on the ritual calendar.

  11. 2018capital

    VW de México fires three hail cannons at storm clouds over its plant in Puebla. Farmers nearby blame them for a dry season. See the hail cannon →

  12. Nowscience

    Satellites image every cloud over Mexico every 10 minutes. See them on the globe →
