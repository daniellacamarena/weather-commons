Two ways to touch a cloud

Horton’s hydrologic cycle (1931) as the baseline · the same numbered steps on each case · Puebla, Mexico

On the left is the water cycle as Robert E. Horton drew it in 1931, with no owners and no interventions. On the right is the same cycle where people have tried to change the weather. One is a hail cannon a car plant fired at storm clouds in 2018. The other is the tiemperos, who have called the rain at the volcanoes for centuries. Choose a step to see what each one does there.

Hail cannon · VW PueblaTiemperos · the volcanoes
Who actsThe car plant. Three cannons fired automatically when a weather radar saw a cloud building.Tiemperos, graniceros or aureros: village specialists chosen by lightning or by dreams.
What they do to the cloudPush it away. One acetylene blast about every 7 seconds.Invite it in. They climb to caves and crosses, feed the volcano and pray.
Who benefitsThe plant. New cars were parked in the open.The rain-fed milpa of the villages: maize, beans and squash.
Who bears the costFarmers nearby, who said about 2,000 ha of maize were lost.The community, with the climb, the offerings and the food.
TimeOne season: May to 20 August 2018.A ritual year from March to November, repeated every year.
KnowledgeEngineering and radar automation, sold as a product.Ritual, dreams and reading the clouds, passed on in the community.
Horton stepsClaims to act at 3–4. The losses show up at 4, 8 and 10. Its paved lot adds 9.Claims to act at 3–4. The ritual follows the cycle from 2 to 12, from the cloud to the springs.
EvidenceStudies have found no measurable effect of hail cannons on hail or rain. July 2018 rain at the plant was 20–35% of normal.Not shown to change a cloud. The practice itself is documented by ethnography (Glockner).
